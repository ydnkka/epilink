## Results

### Diagnostic setting and relationship prevalence

We examined synthetic diagnostics on a fixed transmission tree containing 5,051 fully sampled cases, yielding 12,753,775 unordered case pairs in each of three development observation realizations. The analyses separated ambiguity in the observed pairwise features from the consequences of converting known transmission relationships into disjoint clusters. Relationship horizon M was defined as the number of intervening cases for ancestor–descendant pairs and the sum of the intervening cases on the two branches from the most recent common infector for collateral pairs. Consequently, M=0 includes both direct transmission and shared-infector relationships. Target pairs were rare: 17,652 (0.1384%), 51,395 (0.4030%), and 104,488 (0.8193%) pairs satisfied M=0, M≤1, and M≤2, respectively.

### Temporal distance reduced feature mixing but did not eliminate ambiguity

Adding temporal distance reduced the fraction of occupied feature cells containing both target and non-target pairs at every endpoint and under both distance processes (Fig. 1A–C). Under the deterministic-distance process, mixed-cell fractions for GD alone were 13.90%, 17.44%, and 20.98% for M=0, M≤1, and M≤2; the corresponding GD+TD fractions were 2.12%, 3.76%, and 5.44%. Under the stochastic-distance process, the GD-only fractions were higher, at 18.27%, 22.99%, and 27.58%, and declined to 2.46%, 4.44%, and 6.50% with GD+TD. The stochastic process therefore retained more mixed cells than the deterministic process for every matched feature set and endpoint.

The reduction in cell-level mixing was accompanied by a reduction in the fraction of pairs occupying mixed cells. For M=0, this pair-weighted fraction fell from 3.75% to 1.65% under the deterministic process and from 14.48% to 4.01% under the stochastic process when TD was added. Nevertheless, **all target pairs remained in mixed cells** for every endpoint, feature set, and process. Thus, temporal information separated many non-target pairs from the ambiguous region without creating feature cells containing only targets. Consistent with this residual overlap, the minimum empirical misclassification rate for decisions constant within GD+TD cells remained positive: 0.1192% for deterministic distances and 0.1362% for stochastic distances at M=0. These errors are small relative to all pairs but substantial relative to the 0.1384% prevalence of M=0 targets.

### Exact relationship graphs still exhibited a partitioning trade-off

Leiden community detection did not simultaneously recover all target pairs and exclude all non-target pairs, despite receiving exact endpoint-specific target edges (Fig. 1D–F). Among the resolutions examined, the highest F1 occurred at CPM resolution 0.4 for all three endpoints: 0.892 for M=0, 0.755 for M≤1, and 0.757 for M≤2. At these settings, precision was 0.996, 0.817, and 0.857, while recall was 0.808, 0.702, and 0.678, respectively. M≥3 contamination was 0%, 0.21%, and 14.31%, showing that similar F1 values could accompany markedly different inclusion of distant relationships.

Increasing resolution generally improved precision and reduced contamination at the cost of recall. For M=0, resolutions 0.5–0.9 produced precision of 1.000, recall of 0.804, and F1 of 0.891. At resolution 0.9, precision also approached one for M≤1 and M≤2, but recall fell to 0.462 and 0.524. At resolution 1.0, the implementation returned 5,051 singleton clusters for every endpoint: recall and F1 were zero, while precision and contamination were undefined because no pairs were co-clustered. The reported F1 maxima describe the evaluated development grid; within each resolution, the five Leiden restarts were selected by the CPM objective rather than by truth-based performance.

Connected components, shown as isolated CC reference points in Fig. 1D–F, grouped all 5,051 cases into a single cluster at each of the three relationship horizons. Thus, even the strictest endpoint graph remained connected across the entire outbreak and did not separate recent transmission neighbourhoods.

### Known transmission topology did not yield a common optimal tree threshold

TreeCluster performance depended strongly on both the clustering rule and the relationship endpoint (Fig. 1G–O). For `avg_clade`, the maximum observed F1 was 0.470 at three hops for M=0, and 0.499 and 0.485 at four hops for M≤1 and M≤2. For `max_clade`, the corresponding maxima were 0.433 at four hops, 0.458 at eight hops, and 0.482 at twelve hops. These maxima were below those observed for Leiden on the endpoint-specific oracle graphs, although the two controls use different representations of the known relationships.

The threshold–contamination trade-off also differed between the tree methods. At their M=0 F1 maxima, M≥3 contamination was 5.39% for `avg_clade` and 0.22% for `max_clade`. At the M≤2 maxima, contamination reached 25.39% and 55.90%, respectively. Increasing the threshold to sixteen hops raised contamination to 96.26% for `avg_clade` and 71.90% for `max_clade`. In contrast, `single_linkage` joined all 5,051 cases into one cluster at every tested positive threshold, beginning at one hop. This gave recall of one but M≥3 contamination of 99.18% and F1 values of only 0.0028, 0.0080, and 0.0163 for the three endpoints.

## Discussion

These diagnostics identify two distinct obstacles to recovering transmission relationships from pairwise observations. First, target and non-target pairs can share the same observed genetic and temporal distances. Second, even exact pairwise relationships may not admit a faithful representation as a disjoint partition. The first obstacle concerns the information available to a pairwise decision rule; the second concerns the structure imposed by clustering. Both must be considered when interpreting downstream clustering performance.

The benefit of temporal information was consistent across the three relationship horizons, but the magnitude of the reduction in mixed-cell fraction should not be interpreted as an equivalent reduction in classification error. Introducing TD changes the number and occupancy of feature cells, whereas the plotted fraction weights each occupied cell equally. The accompanying reduction in pair-weighted mixing supports an improvement in empirical separation, but every target pair still shared its feature cell with at least one non-target pair. Perfect pairwise classification is therefore impossible for rules that assign the same binary decision to all pairs with identical GD and TD in these realizations. This is an empirical limitation of these features and their saved resolution, rather than a population-wide performance ceiling. Additional epidemiological covariates or information from the wider network could distinguish pairs that GD and TD alone cannot separate.

The rarity of target pairs makes this distinction particularly important. A rule that rejected every pair would already achieve an error rate of only 0.1384% for M=0, despite recovering no targets. The low absolute feature-only error rates must therefore be interpreted alongside target prevalence, precision, and recall. Similarly, the fixed M≥3 contamination measure captures the inclusion of distant relationships, but it does not include every false positive under M=0 or M≤1. Zero M≥3 contamination can coexist with imperfect precision at these stricter endpoints. Reporting both endpoint-specific precision and distant-pair contamination distinguishes nearby errors from more extensive over-clustering.

The oracle-graph results demonstrate why accurate pairwise edges alone are insufficient to guarantee accurate clusters. A partition implies that every pair within each cluster is selected, whereas the relationship endpoints need not be transitive. For example, adjacent direct-transmission links along a chain can both satisfy M=0 even though the pair spanning the two links does not. A disjoint clustering must either separate some target pairs or include additional non-target pairs. The observed Leiden precision–recall trade-off is consistent with this structural mismatch, alongside the particular CPM objective and its optimization. These results do not establish a theoretical optimum or a universal upper bound for clustering; they show the performance of one community-detection procedure given exact target edges.

Connected components is better matched to delineating independent outbreaks than to identifying recent transmission neighbourhoods within an outbreak. Under the assumption that each outbreak forms a single transmission tree, complete and correctly specified transmission links make each tree a connected component, provided no edges bridge independent outbreaks. However, unsampled cases can interrupt the connecting paths in the observed graph and split one underlying outbreak into several components; conversely, erroneous links between outbreaks can merge them. Component membership therefore depends on both sampling coverage and the edge definition. More importantly, connectivity imposes no limit on the number of successive links between two cases: a chain of individually recent relationships can join cases that are distant in transmission history. This explains why all cases belonged to one component in the fully sampled oracle controls. Connected components alone is consequently unsuitable for delimiting neighbourhoods defined by a bounded recent relationship horizon, even when its individual edges are correct; that task requires additional within-component partitioning or explicit constraints on within-group relationships.

The tree controls provide a complementary illustration. Relationship horizon M is not identical to total path length: for a path of h transmission edges, M=h−1 for ancestor–descendant pairs and M=h−2 for collateral pairs. A single hop threshold therefore does not encode the same relationship criterion for all pairs. The endpoint-dependent F1 maxima and increasing contamination at larger thresholds show the practical consequences of this mismatch, together with the constraints of each TreeCluster method. The single-linkage collapse illustrates the additional effect of chaining: short links can connect cases whose mutual relationship is distant. Performance differences between these tree controls and oracle Leiden should not be read as a direct comparison of inferred-phylogeny clustering and feature-based community detection, because the controls receive different truth-derived inputs.

Finally, these findings are conditional on one fully sampled transmission tree and the configured observation processes. The three development seeds describe variation in observations on that fixed structure; graph and tree controls are reused across seeds and do not constitute independent epidemic replicates. The tree control also excludes phylogenetic reconstruction error by using known transmission topology rather than a molecular genealogy. The diagnostics support joint evaluation of pairwise information, partitioning choices, relationship horizon, and contamination. Establishing how broadly these patterns extend requires evaluation across additional transmission structures, sampling fractions, and observation settings, with operating-point choices assessed on held-out data.
